## Article summary
A recent internal memo from Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) reveals that the agency is authorizing its officers to forcibly enter homes to make arrests without a judge's warrant. This directive represents a significant departure from longstanding guidance that respected constitutional limits on government searches, which generally require a judicial warrant for home entry. The memo permits ICE officers to use force to enter a residence based on a narrower administrative warrant for individuals with a final order of removal. Advocates argue this policy conflicts with Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable searches and seizures. The shift coincides with the Trump administration's expansion of immigration arrests nationwide. For years, immigrant advocates advised individuals not to open their doors unless presented with a judge-signed warrant, advice now undercut by this new ICE directive. The memo has reportedly been used in training for new ICE officers, even contradicting existing written training materials. While the extent of its application is unclear, an AP report documented ICE officers ramming through a Minneapolis home's door with only an administrative warrant. The Department of Homeland Security states that individuals with administrative warrants have already undergone full due process. However, the memo's legal basis and implications are being questioned, and the policy is expected to face legal challenges and criticism from advocacy groups.
