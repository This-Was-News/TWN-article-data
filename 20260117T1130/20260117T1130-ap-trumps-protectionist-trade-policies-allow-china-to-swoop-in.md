## Article summary
President Donald Trump's protectionist trade policies are prompting his partners to seek business elsewhere, with Canada recently slashing import taxes on Chinese electric vehicles in exchange for lower tariffs on its farm products. This move signifies a major realignment in Canada's economic relations, as the perceived economic threat from the U.S. now outweighs that from China. Canada has frequently been a target of Trump's tariffs, impacting sectors like steel and aluminum. The recent deal with China, however, carries risks for Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who might face retribution from Trump ahead of crucial North American trade pact negotiations. Other trade partners are also diversifying; the European Union is finalizing a pact with Mercosur and pursuing a deal with India, while China has successfully shifted exports away from the U.S. to markets like Europe and Southeast Asia, resulting in a record trade surplus. Trump's administration has imposed broad tariffs, aiming to protect American industries and encourage investment, though his use of these measures is often arbitrary. Canada's deal with China offers benefits to its canola farmers and acknowledges China's strengths in the EV sector, a move that counters the Trump administration's less favorable stance on green energy. Despite potential criticism and risks to Canadian auto workers, the EV import quotas are limited. The most significant risk for Canada, however, stems from the upcoming renewal of the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), where Trump may demand changes or threaten withdrawal, potentially retaliating against Canada for its deal with China. American businesses, reliant on the USMCA, may push back against Trump's demands. Canada's deal with China signals a willingness to explore other partnerships and provides leverage in upcoming trade talks.
